## Problem
LLM guardrails are a "fragmented ecosystem" with no unified taxonomy or evaluation framework; current evaluations "predominantly focus on raw defense efficacy" and overlook "computational cost (e.g., inference latency, GPU resource consumption) and utility (e.g., the rate of misclassifying benign prompts as malicious)" (Sec 1, p.1). Many guardrails lack universality across LLMs, attack types and deployment contexts (Sec 1, p.1).

## Research question(s)
Four RQs embedded in Sec 4 and answered in Sec 6:
- RQ1 (Sec 4.1/6, p.13): how does the intervention stage (pre-/intra-/post-processing) affect delay?
- RQ2 (Sec 4.2, p.9; answered p.13–14): how does the technical paradigm (rule-/model-/LLM-based) affect GPU memory footprint?
- RQ3 (Sec 4.3, p.9; answered p.13): are session-level guardrails effective against multi-turn jailbreaks?
- RQ4 (Sec 4.3, p.9; answered p.14): how does safety granularity (token/sequence/session) affect utility (FPR)?
Plus a cross-attack generalisation check on prompt injection (Sec 6, p.14).

## Method / architecture / algorithm
- **Taxonomy** with six dimensions (Sec 3.3, p.4–5): intervention stage (pre/intra/post-processing), technical paradigm (rule-based, model-based, LLM-based), safety granularity (token/sequence/session), reactiveness (static/dynamic), applicability (white-box/black-box), explainability. Formal guardrail definition: Assess(G_R, X) ∈ {allow, block}; a jailbreak succeeds only if both the target LLM's alignment and the guardrail are bypassed (Eqs. 2–4, Sec 3.2, p.4).
- **SEU evaluation framework** (Sec 3.4, p.4–5): Security = Attack Success Rate (ASR, attacks that bypass guardrail *and* elicit harmful output) and Pass Guardrail Rate (PGR, attacks the guardrail alone lets through); Efficiency = Extra Delay = T(guardrail+LLM) − T(LLM) averaged over responses (Eq. 5, p.5) and GPU Memory Overhead = peak M(guardrail+LLM) − M(LLM) (Eq. 6, p.5); Utility = FPR on benign queries (p.5).
- **Leaderboard** (Sec 5.3, p.13; Fig. 3): Mean-ASR and Mean-PGR over nine attacks, plus Extra Delay, GPU memory and FPR measured on OR-Bench; each metric min-max normalised and inverted; composite = average of five scores.

## Dataset(s) (+ size, licence) / benchmark
Table 2 (p.10): JailbreakHub 1,000 in-the-wild prompts (IJP); JailbreakBench 100 harmful instructions driving GCG, AutoDAN, TAP, LLM-Fuzzer, DrAttack, X-Teaming; MultiJail 315 Bengali prompts; SafeMTData 600 ActorAttack multi-turn queries; benign: AlpacaEval 805 prompts and OR-Bench 1,000 sampled "seemingly toxic but benign" prompts. Target LLMs: Llama-3-8B-Instruct (main text), Vicuna-13b-v1.5 (App. A), GPT-4-0125-Preview (App. B) (Sec 5.1, p.10). Judge: GPT-4o scoring 1–5, success = 5; human validation on 100 samples: agreement 0.9533, Fleiss' kappa 0.9319 (Sec 7.1, p.15). Cross-attack set: 203 samples from Hugging Face "deepset/prompt-injections" (Sec 6, p.14). Licences: Not reported.

## Baselines
Guardrails evaluated (Sec 5.1 "Baselines", p.11): Perplexity Filter (Llama-2-7b perplexity, threshold = max perplexity in JailbreakBench), SmoothLLM (10% character swaps), Llama Guard (Llama-2-7b fine-tune; Pre and Post variants), GradSafe, GradientCuff, SelfDefend (Direct and Intent prompts), WildGuard (Pre/Post), Prompt Guard (Prompt-Guard-86M classifier), GuardReasoner (Pre/Post). No guardrail = Llama-3-8B-Instruct alone.

## Metrics
ASR, PGR, Extra Delay (s), GPU Memory Overhead (GB), FPR; composite SEU score (Sec 3.4 p.4–5; Sec 5.3 p.13).

Latency (Extra Delay, seconds, Fig. 2(a) p.12 and Fig. 4 p.13; values are plotted, not tabulated):
- Text statements: "Perplexity Filter, Llama Guard, SelfDefend and PromptGuard stand out with negligible latency"; "GuardReasoner and GradientCuff impose the most significant delays, with GuardReasoner (Post) being particularly notable"; "The majority of other guardrails maintain an additional delay generally not exceeding 0.5 seconds" (Sec 5.2 Efficiency, p.12). Pre-processing guardrails "generally introduce negligible, or in some cases even negative, additional latency"; for identical detection models post-processing variants "consistently incur greater delay" (e.g., WildGuard (Post) > WildGuard (Pre)) (Sec 6 RQ1, p.13).
- OURS approximate readings from the Fig. 2(a) chart (y-axis −0.5 to 2.5 s): GuardReasoner (Pre) ≈2.0 s and GuardReasoner (Post) ≈2.1–2.2 s; GradientCuff ≈1.6 s on AlpacaEval and ≈0.5 s on OR-Bench; WildGuard (Post) ≈0.5 s; Prompt Guard, Perplexity Filter, Llama Guard (Pre/Post), SelfDefend ≈0–0.25 s. These are visual estimates, not printed numbers — do not cite as exact.
- Millisecond-level latencies are NOT reported; the finest resolution is the 0.5 s scale of the plot.

GPU memory (Fig. 2(b), p.12; text Sec 6 RQ2, p.13–14): SmoothLLM zero additional memory; GradientCuff and PromptGuard "near-zero"; GradSafe higher than several LLM-based methods; GuardReasoner highest. OURS approximate chart readings: GuardReasoner ≈45 GB, GradSafe ≈30 GB, Llama Guard/SelfDefend/WildGuard ≈15–20 GB (estimates only).

FPR on benign inputs (Fig. 2(c), p.12; text Sec 5.2 Utility p.12 and RQ4 p.14): SelfDefend (Direct) highest on OR-Bench at 0.221; GradientCuff highest on AlpacaEval at 0.083; SmoothLLM, SelfDefend (Intent), WildGuard (Pre), GuardReasoner (Pre) also comparatively high; WildGuard (Pre) > 10% on OR-Bench vs WildGuard (Post) < 5%; session-level ("Post") variants consistently lower FPR than sequence-level ("Pre") variants of the same model. OURS approximate chart reading for Prompt Guard: ≈0.02 on AlpacaEval, ≈0.10 on OR-Bench (visual estimate).

Leaderboard (Fig. 3, p.13; Sec 5.3): Prompt Guard has the highest composite SEU score "but its low M-PGR suggests potential gaps in detection robustness"; GuardReasoner (Pre) best defence "at a significant cost to efficiency and utility"; SelfDefend (Intent) balanced but higher FPR. No single guardrail dominates.

Cross-attack / prompt injection (Fig. 5, p.14; 203 deepset/prompt-injections samples, PGR): Llama Guard (Pre) 0.911; SelfDefend (intent) 0.749; GuardReasoner (Pre) 0.680; WildGuard (Pre) 0.655; SelfDefend (direct) 0.512. Guardrails were not subverted into producing arbitrary output, but their ability to filter injections is "modest at best" (Sec 6, p.14).

Throughput, cost: Not reported.

## Stated limitations (with location)
Sec 7.1, p.14–15: efficiency measured on NVIDIA H800 only; absolute latency/memory "may vary across different hardware platforms and optimization techniques such as quantization" though relative trends expected to hold; reliance on GPT-4o as judge (validated on 100 samples by three PhD students). Sec 3.4 (p.5): the three qualitative taxonomy dimensions (reactiveness, applicability, explainability) are not evaluated empirically.

## Limitations we observe (labelled OURS)
- OURS: Extra Delay is reported only graphically at 0.5-second resolution and as the *difference* between guardrail+LLM and LLM-alone end-to-end response time; guardrail-only inference time, distributions/percentiles, batch size, sequence lengths and number of GPUs are not given, so sub-100 ms comparisons (as in P61/P63) cannot be made from this paper.
- OURS: Efficiency is measured on datacenter H800 GPUs with an 8B target LLM co-resident; CPU-only or edge deployment (typical for a web-application validation layer) is not considered.
- OURS: Prompt Guard and the other pre-processing filters are evaluated only as jailbreak/harm detectors; the prompt-injection cross-check uses 203 samples and reports PGR only for LLM-based guardrails (Prompt Guard is absent from Fig. 5).
- OURS: FPR is measured on chatbot-style prompts (AlpacaEval, OR-Bench), not on application-structured or web-form inputs.
- OURS (scope): the SoK covers jailbreak guardrails for LLM prompts/responses; it does not address validation of non-LLM web request payloads. Its relevance to us is the SEU framing and the empirical latency/FPR trade-off pattern, not the specific numbers.

## Relevance to our RQs
- **RQ5 (semantic-layer detection):** ADJACENT. Provides the taxonomy vocabulary (pre-processing, model-based, sequence-level, static, black-box) that positions a lightweight semantic classifier such as Prompt Guard or an embedding+classifier layer, and shows model-based pre-processing filters achieve "negligible" delay and near-zero memory but weaker PGR (Table 3, Fig. 2, Fig. 3).
- **RQ6 (latency/overhead):** PARTIAL. Establishes Extra Delay and GPU Memory Overhead as formal efficiency metrics (Eqs. 5–6, p.5) and the empirical pattern that LLM-based/reasoning guardrails add ~2 s while classifier-based pre-filters add ~0 s on H800 (Fig. 2(a)); but no millisecond data.
- **RQ7 (trade-off):** DIRECT at the framework level: the SEU trifecta and composite leaderboard are exactly a security–efficiency–utility trade-off analysis; the finding that no guardrail dominates (Sec 5.3, p.13) supports treating our validation layer as an operating-point decision.
- **FPR on benign inputs:** Explicitly a first-class "Utility" metric; range across guardrails 0–0.221 on OR-Bench and up to 0.083 on AlpacaEval (Sec 5.2, p.12); session-level context lowers FPR (RQ4, p.14).

## Potential gap / novelty evidence (quote LOCATION; short quotes only)
- Sec 1, p.1: evaluations "frequently overlooking crucial factors such as computational cost (e.g., inference latency, GPU resource consumption) and utility" — supports the gap that overhead and FPR of input guardrails are under-measured.
- Sec 3.4, p.4–5: introduction of Extra Delay and GPU Overhead as evaluation metrics — a precedent we can adopt and extend to per-request ms and CPU settings.
- Sec 6, p.14: cross-attack generalisation is "a crucial gap in the current state of guardrail technology"; guardrails' injection-filtering capability "is modest at best".
- Sec 7.1, p.14–15: absolute latency "may vary across different hardware platforms" — motivates hardware-controlled measurement in our RQ6.
- Absence evidence: no ms-resolution latency, no CPU deployment, no structured-input benign set.

## Detector design details useful for our semantic-layer candidates
- Prompt Guard: Meta's Prompt-Guard-86M lightweight classifier (Sec 5.1, p.11–12); in this SoK it has near-zero extra delay and near-zero GPU memory overhead on H800 (Fig. 2(a)(b), text p.12–14) with mean ASR 0.163 / mean PGR 0.597 (Table 3, p.11).
- Model-based paradigm examples (Sec 4.2, p.8): KNN (RigorLLM), LightGBM (RSAA), Random Forest on pretrained embeddings [89], MLP heads (HSF, CURVALID), fine-tuned mDeBERTa (PromptGuard) and DistilBERT (erase-and-check) — a catalogue of the same "embedding + light classifier" design space we are evaluating.
- Latency measurement method: Extra Delay = mean end-to-end response time with guardrail minus without (Eq. 5, p.5), measured on benign AlpacaEval/OR-Bench inputs and per attack type (Fig. 4, p.13); GPU memory = peak difference (Eq. 6, p.5). Hardware H800, PyTorch 2.6.0 (Sec 5.1, p.12). Batch size, sequence length, repetitions: Not reported.
