### Summary of the paper (as this reviewer understands it)

The paper uses a frozen Next.js/Zod testbed: 7 input surfaces, 39 fields, and 43 written business rules, of which 5 are enforced in code. It submits inputs from seven generation conditions (A–G) through the real HTTP path. Semantic validity is decided by 18 objective rule functions plus a judge model (gemma3:4b, per the raw data; the paper does not name it). The main results are as follows:

- H1 is supported: 28.0 % of decided, structurally valid LLM inputs are SV-SI.
- H2 is supported: rates vary by category.
- H3a–c are not supported. Random structurally valid inputs are more often SV-SI (54.6 %) than LLM inputs, both pooled and within target.
- H5 is not supported: the best non-judge layer reaches 26.9 % recall at about 6 % FPR.

The authors' own reading is that "the gap is not specific to AI" (Sec. IX, RQ3).

### Strengths

- The design is pre-registered. Deviations are logged and negative results are reported plainly (H3a–c, H5). This is rare and valuable.
- The paper separates the schema verdict from the application verdict, and conditional from unconditional rates. This is a real methodological improvement over acceptance-oracle studies (P20, P21).
- The Introduction and Related Work are honest that the phenomenon is known for outputs (P33–P37). The paper does not claim discovery.
- The random, rule-based and boundary baselines are exactly what let the authors refute the AI-specific hypothesis. That is a genuine contribution.

### Novelty assessment after results

- **Kill search, 2026-09-22.** Four WebSearch queries on LLM-generated form inputs, semantic validation layers for web inputs, and LLM vs random vs human semantic validity. No work was found that measures structurally-valid-but-semantically-invalid rates at a web application's input boundary across AI and non-AI producers, or that evaluates business-rule validators on CPU. The closest new items (tool-call parameter benchmarks, above) are output-side.
- **Novelty now rests on:**
  - (i) the controlled cross-producer comparison and its negative result;
  - (ii) the input-boundary measurement protocol, which separates the schema verdict from the application verdict;
  - (iii) the negative detection result for cheap layers on business-rule validity;
  - (iv) the CPU cost, after E4.
- **Weakened claims:** C1, which is AI-specific framing; C2, where no taxonomy is presented; and the human-reference part of C3.
- **Overall novelty risk: MEDIUM.** The novelty is honest if it is reframed. It would be HIGH if the paper kept the AI-specific framing, because the paper's own data refute it.

### Simulated recommendation: Major revision

Main reasons:
1. The headline LLM SV-SI rate pools prompts that instruct the model to break rules, without saying so (I-R2-001).
2. The title, Introduction and contribution list still frame a result the data refuted. Two advertised elements are not delivered: the C2 taxonomy and the C4 benign-unusual FPR (I-R2-002, -003, -004, -009).
3. The ground-truth judge is unnamed and unvalidated, which limits every judgement-rule and RQ5 claim (I-R2-005).
4. There is one factual misattribution of a nearest-neighbour result (I-R2-006), and several claims about related work are worded more strongly than the sources that were read.

The design, the transparency, and the negative results are strong. With reframing, these become the paper's contribution.

### Guidance for the Abstract and Conclusion (to be written last)

**Must say:**
- The gap exists at the input boundary: 28.0 % (CI 25.6–30.6) of decided structurally valid model inputs broke a business rule, **pooled over prompt families including requests for rule-violating values**. Also report the legitimate-value (P1) rate once it is produced by a saved script.
- The gap is not specific to AI. Random structurally valid inputs violated rules more often (54.6 %). Context (purpose plus rules) reduced the model's violation odds (OR 0.25, within target, post hoc).
- The most frequent violation was an objective rule (a date in the past) that the application could enforce in code. On AI-facing fields most violations came from ordinary prompts, not injection.
- No candidate layer met the pre-registered target (best non-judge 26.9 % recall at 6.1 % FPR). Prompted models discriminate better (AUROC 0.75–0.77) but are coarse and unstable across fields.
- RQ6 latency, only as measured.

**Must state as limitations:**
- one application;
- A and G AI-drafted, so there is no AI-versus-human comparison yet;
- judge–human κ not yet reported;
- the judge is a 4B model;
- CPU-only, small quantised models.

**Must not say:**
- that AI-generated inputs are more dangerous or more likely to be SV-SI than other inputs;
- "human" for conditions A and G without the AI-drafted qualifier;
- "deployable" for a layer that missed the target;
- "significant" without a test;
- "first";
- any generalisation beyond the single testbed;
- any latency figure before E4 exists;
- that the judge ground truth is validated.

**The abstract must not open with the AI-specific premise.** Open with the boundary, e.g.: "Schema validation checks whether an input is well formed, not whether it is appropriate. We measure how often inputs pass a web application's schema while breaking its business rules, for model-generated and non-model inputs alike ..."
